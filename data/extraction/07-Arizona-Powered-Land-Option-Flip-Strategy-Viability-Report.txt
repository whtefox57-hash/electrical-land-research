Executive Summary — The Verdict
The strategy points at a real market ineﬀiciency , but the version described cannot capture
it at a $100k budget in 12–24 months. It is viable only in a modified form: assign the option
rather than close, sell diligence rather than power, and treat 1.2–1.6x (or a flat assignment
fee) as the realistic outcome — not 2–5x.
The core flaw is one of kind, not degree. The 2–5x (and larger) multiples observed in real transactions are
paid for utility commitments — a will-serve letter, an executed service or interconnection agreement,
a queue position with a credible energization date — layered on top of entitlements. A consultant-
prepared “electrical report” showing theoretical capacity is neither of those things. Buyers in this market
are explicitly trained to discount it: as one land brokerage that specializes in data-center sites puts it,
the premium attaches to parcels where the power delivery timeline is backed by the utility, because “sites
with available power and a utility Letter of Authorization already in place command a premium because
the interconnection process — often the longest and most expensive part of development — has already
been completed. ” (QuoteColo) What you are describing is the beginning of the value-creation ladder, and
the market pays for the top of it.
Three findings from this research compound the problem, each specific to your own uploaded work. First,
the crown jewels of your screen — Cholla, Springerville, and Coronado, your top three sites — are no

1. What the Strategy Gets Right
Before dissecting the failure modes, credit where due: the underlying thesis — that power access, not
land, is the scarce asset in data-center development, and that scarcity is monetizable — is
not only correct, it is the single most documented fact in the 2025–2026 market. CBRE’s H2 2025 North
America report states that “grid-power capacity for existing projects is largely booked through 2030
in most markets,” that “sites offering power access within 18 to 36 months for greenfield development
are highly sought after,” and that power scarcity is “escalating site costs,” with recent transactions in
Northern Virginia and the Northeast exceeding $8 million per acre . (CBRE) JLL’s year-end 2025 report
records vacancy at 1% for a second consecutive year, 92% of under-construction capacity precommitted,
and 64% of the construction pipeline now in “frontier” markets — exactly the secondary and tertiary
geography your screen targets. (JLL) Phoenix specifically has become the fourth-largest North American
market, with vacancy cut in half to 1.7%, 112.4 MW of net absorption, and land prices appreciating
40–50% year-over-year — the fastest of any secondary market. (CBRE)
The structure of the play is also institutionally recognized rather than exotic. CBRE IM’s taxonomy of
data-center investment strategies names “powered land” explicitly: “the investor acquires land, completes
entitlements, secures the provision of power and then sells the shovel-ready site,” avoiding operational risk
in exchange for giving up recurring cash flow. (CBRE Investment Management) And the instrument you
chose — the option-to-purchase — is exactly what professional developers use at the site-evaluation stage:
the Bipartisan Policy Center’s development-cycle overview describes developers pursuing “land-control
agreements, such as purchase options or letters of intent” while they evaluate power, water, fiber, and
permitting. (Bipartisan Policy Center) Your instinct to control land cheaply while proving up its power
story is, in other words, the industry-standard first move.
The insight embedded in your uploaded screen is also genuinely differentiated. You correctly identified
that retired coal plants carry existing high-voltage interconnection — the single hardest thing to replicate
— and that Arizona’s patchwork of ACC-regulated IOUs, SRP, co-ops, and irrigation districts creates
information asymmetry that a diligent researcher can exploit before institutional capital arrives. The
problem is not the thesis, the geography, or the instrument. The problem is the value step the plan
actually performs versus the value step the market pays a multiple for — and that is where the rest
of this report concentrates.

3. The “Electrical Report” Problem
3.1 A consultant’s report is not capacity , and the market knows it
The pivotal assumption in the plan is that an “electrical report showing the land can handle large electric
capacity” is a saleable value-add. The market evidence says buyers treat such documents as marketing
material to be independently re-verified , not as de-risking. The specialist brokerage guidance for
MW-scale listings instructs buyers to ask of any claimed available load: “Is this live today, or contingent
on a Letter of Authorization that takes months? Ask for the interconnection agreement” — and of any
substation-distance claim: “Verify the substation has available capacity and ask about the utility queue. ”
(QuoteColo) A seller-side legal guide is equally blunt: “In this sector, power is often the asset. Sellers who
assume proximity equals deliverability may misprice the site or negotiate from false premises. ” (lowndes-
law.com) Your own screen already proved the underlying point from the data side: HIFLD publishes
no MV A or MW rating for any substation, so 248 of your 255 candidates carry null capacity , and
proximity-plus-voltage is an availability signal, not a capacity signal — a 500 kV substation with no
spare transformer capacity is worth less than a 230 kV one with headroom, and no public dataset can
tell them apart.
There is a second, subtler problem: even if a competent engineering firm models the transmission system
correctly, the answer it produces is theoretical capability , not permission . Servable load depends
on transformer bank ratings, conductor limits, protection schemes, the utility’s planning criteria, and —
decisively in 2026 — the utility’s commercial willingness to commit, none of which a consultant controls.
Utilities themselves are drowning in speculative requests: APS alone has roughly 10 GW of pending
data-center interconnection requests it says it cannot commit to serve without risking reliability for

3.2 The real thing costs real money — and expires
Obtaining the document that does move value — a utility load study, capacity commitment, or service
agreement — has a published price structure that sits awkwardly against a $100k total budget. Georgia
Power’s data-center tariff settlement established tiered study fees of $10,000 / $50,000 / $100,000
scaled to the size of the capacity request. (CoBank) Texas SB 6 floors the initial transmission screening
study fee at $100,000, adds per-megawatt financial-security obligations on the order of $50,000/MW in
draft PUCT rules, and requires proof of site control plus disclosure of parallel requests — an explicit
anti-phantom-load mechanism. (Compute Law Blog) On the generation side of APS’s own tariff, the
fee stack is a $100,000 study deposit, a $5,000 non-refundable application fee, and a further $200,000
commercial-readiness payment — directional evidence of what “sitting at the table” costs in APS territory.
(zeroemissiongrid.com) Portland General’s process illustrates the structure: a non-refundable application
fee plus a reconciled study deposit, with the completed study expiring 60–90 days after issuance
unless extended. (PGE Oregon)
The expiry point deserves emphasis because it inverts the flip logic. The plan calls for obtaining the
electrical validation first and selling later, but a utility study is a perishable good tied to a specific ap-
plicant’s load request. Commission it at month 3 of your option and it may be stale by month 6; the
credible buyer at month 14 inherits an expired study and must restart — at which point your report
adds nothing the buyer’s own process wouldn’t produce. Meanwhile the queue position and any capacity
reservation attach to the applicant’s account, site control, and financial commitments; under the emerg-
ing federal framework (FERC’s large-load rulemaking, expected final around June 2026), load customers
face standardized deposits, readiness proofs, withdrawal penalties, and 100% responsibility for assigned
network-upgrade costs. (White & Case LLP) The 2025–2026 policy direction — from DOE’s Section 403
directive to PJM’s co-location carve-outs to Arizona’s own HB 2756 — is uniformly toward hardening
commitments and shrinking speculative optionality , as Janus Henderson’s sector analysis notes:
“Regulatory regimes are moving toward longer-term take-or-pay structures and direct infrastructure con-
tributions. This could reduce speculative optionality and concentrate value in committed assets. ” (Janus
Henderson Investors) The system is being redesigned, in real time, against your exact business model.
3.3 Can a land-flipper even request the study?
A final mechanical issue: large-load studies are customer processes. The utility is evaluating a load —
a facility with a defined demand profile, ramp schedule, load factor, and credit posture — not a parcel.

proposes re-scoping the tariff to ￿5 MW data centers with direct assignment of new-generation

agreements requiring “up-front financial support. ” (Arizona Corporation Commission) A non-end-user

are actively engineering out of their queues. You may get a preliminary feasibility conversation; you will

93.64) ceased coal generation in March 2025; APS has since announced it will convert two units to natural

SRP’s Unit 4 (approved November 2025, complete by December 2029), with Tri-State’s Unit 3 retiring

plant supplying ~10% of SRP peak — won unanimous ACC approval in March 2026 for gas conversion

Read those paragraphs as a land speculator: the interconnection capacity your model treated
as stranded and available is being re-absorbed by the incumbent utilities for their own
generation. The sites will not be sold; the substations will not have the implied headroom; and the
comparison to Homer City, Pennsylvania — the marquee coal-to-data-center conversion, where the plant
owner plus Kiewit and GE Vernova are building a 4.4 GW gas campus across 3,200 acres — shows who
captures that value when it does get captured: the party that already owns the asset and can fund turbine
purchases, not an option-holder next door. (homercityredevelopment.com) The version of your thesis that

5.2 The multiple math, honestly
Run the deal on 100 acres at $10k/acre. You option it (say $20k fee), commission the report ($40k),
spend $20k on legal/title/carrying — $80k in, fitting the budget. A buyer who believes your diligence
might pay a 20–50% diligence-and-scarcity premium for an assignable position on a genuinely well-sited
parcel: call it $1.0–1.5M for the property against your $1.0M strike. But you do not have $1.0M to close,
so the monetizable instrument is the option assignment , and option assignments clear at the spread,
typically realized as a fee of 5–20% of contract price — $50k–$200k gross on these numbers, against $80k
of sunk cost, if a buyer materializes inside the option window and if the site’s capacity story survives the
buyer’s own verification. That is a plausible small win or a plausible total loss; it is not a 2–5x return on
anything except, optimistically, the option fee itself.
The 2–5x path requires climbing to rung 3–4 of the ladder: entitlements plus a utility commitment. The
cash cost of that version — land purchase plus studies plus carrying through a multi-year utility process
— starts around $825k and realistically exceeds $1.5–2M once financial-assurance requirements are
honored, with a 3–7 year horizon that matches how professionals actually do it: Tract’s Caldwell County
megasite took an 18-month entitlement engagement before its 1,515-acre acquisition, and its 360 MW
of initial power arrives in 2028 via a Facility Design Agreement with Bluebonnet Electric — three-plus
years from agreement to energization. (Data Center Frontier) Grid interconnection generally runs 3–15
years, substation upgrades 3–7 years. (USP&E) Twelve to twenty-four months is enough time to sell a
story; it is not enough time to build the asset the story is about.
5.3 Timeline cross-check
The 12–24 month window is not impossible — it is simply only compatible with the assignment version.
Negotiating the option (1–4 months), the consultant memo (2–6 months), county zoning confirmation
(2–8 months), and a brokered sale of the position (6–18 months) can all fit inside 24 months, provided
the option is written with an assignment clause and at least one extension. What cannot fit is the version
where value is maximized: any process that depends on a utility queue position, a special-use permit
hearing cycle in a county now regulating data centers, or a rezoning with community engagement runs
on other people’s calendars. Vintage Partners’ abandoned Laveen project is the cautionary Arizona data
point: even a funded developer with a 63-acre metro site walked away in March 2026 when Phoenix’s new
standards and SRP’s clustered large-load queue raised the mitigation and grid-upgrade burden — a
reminder that in 2026, the queue itself is a permitting regime, and it does not respect anyone’s 24-month
option window. (This Is Server Country)
